The Role of Young People in Politics

Use these ten subtitles to plan your essay. Answer each guiding question in your own words and add a specific example. The example sentences are starting points; adapt them to your community and argument.

  1. Introduction: Why Young Voices Matter

    What to write: Introduce politics as decisions that affect a community and state why young people should have a voice.

    Example: “Decisions about schools, transport, and the environment affect young people, so their ideas deserve to be heard.”

  2. What Political Participation Means

    What to write: Explain that participation can include learning about issues, discussing proposals, contacting representatives, and voting when eligible.

    Example: “A teenager who attends a public meeting to ask about local bus routes is taking part in civic life.”

  3. Issues That Affect Young People

    What to write: Choose one or two issues and explain how young people experience them firsthand.

    Example: “Students who rely on buses to reach school can explain what happens when a route is reduced.”

  4. Learning How Decisions Are Made

    What to write: Describe why understanding local and national decision-making helps people propose realistic changes.

    Example: “Before asking for a new crossing near school, students could find out which local body is responsible for road safety.”

  5. Student Councils and Local Action

    What to write: Show how young people can practice representing others and working on community problems.

    Example: “A student council could gather classmates' views and present a plan for safer bike parking.”

  6. Social Media as a Civic Tool

    What to write: Explain how online platforms can share ideas and organize participation while also spreading misleading claims.

    Example: “Before sharing a post about a proposed rule, students could read the original proposal and check its date.”

  7. Respectful Debate and Different Opinions

    What to write: Discuss how listening, asking questions, and using evidence can help people disagree constructively.

    Example: “Two students may disagree about a school policy but still compare its costs and benefits without insulting each other.”

  8. Barriers to Participation

    What to write: Identify an obstacle, such as lack of information, time, or a chance to speak, and suggest a way to address it.

    Example: “A town could schedule a youth forum after school so more students can attend.”

  9. How Adults Can Make Space for Youth

    What to write: Suggest practical ways schools and officials can invite young people's views and show what happened afterward.

    Example: “Local officials could ask young residents about park plans and explain which suggestions they used.”

  10. Conclusion: From Ideas to Action

    What to write: Restate your main argument and summarize meaningful ways young people can contribute.

    Example: “Young people can improve public decisions by learning about issues, sharing their experiences, and working with others.”

Writing tip: Turn each subtitle into a paragraph. State one point, give a concrete example, and explain how it supports your argument. If you mention voting rules, public policies, or statistics, check an up-to-date reliable source for your location and cite it. Combine related sections for a shorter essay.
